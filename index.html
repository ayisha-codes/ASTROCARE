  • Add notes whenever something feels different, even if it seems small.
  • Contact the crew medical officer if you feel unwell or concerned.
  • Alert the crew and follow mission medical protocols for urgent or worsening symptoms.

Space Hazards Awareness

Human spaceflight involves several environmental and operational hazards. The following cards provide brief, general educational summaries of risks that can affect astronaut health and well-being.

☢

Space Radiation

Beyond Earth's protective magnetic field, astronauts are exposed to more radiation. Over long missions, this is a concern for long-term health, so exposure is carefully monitored.

☉

Altered Gravity

Living in microgravity affects the body, including muscles, bones, and the cardiovascular system. Exercise and medical monitoring help the crew adapt.

☀

Isolation and Confinement

Small crews, limited space, and distance from home can affect mood, sleep, and teamwork. Noticing changes early supports well-being and performance.

☁

Hostile, Closed Environments

A spacecraft is a sealed habitat. Air quality, noise, temperature, and cleanliness all shape crew health and need ongoing attention.

📡

Distance from Earth

As missions travel farther from Earth, communication delays can increase. Access to immediate medical support, supplies, and emergency assistance may become more difficult.

Why are these hazards important?

Space hazards can affect physical health, mental well-being, and mission performance. Understanding these risks helps explain why astronauts need regular health monitoring and support throughout a mission.

About AstroCare

The problem

On long-duration space missions, astronauts are far from hospitals and can face delays in communicating with Earth. Environmental hazards, demanding mission conditions, and changes in physical or mental well-being make regular health monitoring important. Small day-to-day changes can be difficult to notice without a consistent record.

Our solution

AstroCare is a browser-based astronaut health monitoring prototype. It provides a simple daily check-in, a history of recorded entries, and trend charts to help users review changes over time. It also provides general health guidance and space hazard awareness.

Why these four health indicators?

We selected heart rate, body temperature, sleep duration, and mood as four simple indicators for an educational daily check-in. Together, they offer a basic snapshot of physical and emotional well-being. Tracking them over time may help users notice changes and decide when to discuss concerns with their medical team.

These indicators are not a complete health assessment and cannot independently determine an astronaut's medical condition.

How AstroCare works

  • Users manually enter their daily health information.
  • Entries are saved in the browser using local storage.
  • Users can review recent check-ins in a history table.
  • Trend charts help users visualize recorded values over time.
  • General guidance encourages communication with the crew medical officer.

Current limitations

  • AstroCare is an educational prototype, not a medical device.
  • It has not been clinically validated.
  • Sample or demonstration values are illustrative, not real astronaut measurements or live NASA data.
  • Data is manually entered and stored only in the current browser.
  • It does not provide diagnosis, automated medical alerts, or a connection to a medical team.

Future possibilities

  • Integration with validated wearable health sensors.
  • Secure data sharing and review by authorized medical teams.
  • Multilingual support for international astronaut crews.
  • Improved communication and follow-up tools for long-duration missions.
  • Further research into reliable health monitoring in space environments.
